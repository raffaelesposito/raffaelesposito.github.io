01

The context

In Italy, people in their twenties are the age group that gives the least blood, and generational turnover is the system's main problem. The interesting part is something else: young people do show up. The challenge is getting them to come back, because the system relies on regular donors.

34.1

donors per 1,000 people aged 18 to 25. Among 46-to-55-year-olds it's 63.6.

+5%

new donors aged 18 to 25 in 2024: more than 75,000. Some people do take the first step.

−0.8%

total donors in 2025, 1,664,691 in all. Turnover isn't keeping up.

Source: Centro Nazionale Sangue (Italy's national blood centre), 2024 and 2025 data.

What someone in their twenties finds today

The first step is a phone call.

To become a donor, the national website asks you to find your local branch and call it.

An app for every branch.

There's no national app: each area has its own tool, almost always designed for people who already donate.

After donating, silence.

No tool tells donors what happened to their blood.

What has worked elsewhere

+10%

more donations when donors get a message naming the hospital their blood went to. Austrian and German Red Cross, study published in 2023.

70% vs 63%

of donors who get that message give again within a year, compared with those who don't. The effect is stronger among new donors. Australian study, 2018.

In Sweden and the UK the service has existed for years. In Italy every blood bag must already be traceable by law, but nobody tells the donor.

02

The insight

We always know where the things we send are. Except our blood.

People in their twenties track everything that leaves them: yesterday's parcel, whether a message has been read, the delivery rider three minutes away. The most personal thing they can give, their own blood, is the only thing that disappears: ten minutes in a chair, a plaster, and then silence. It isn't a lack of generosity. For people who grew up with notifications, a gesture with no feedback feels like half a gesture.

And for coming back

You remember your first donation. Your second gets lost in your calendar.

People who stop after the first time usually haven't changed their minds: they just didn't plan. The next visit should be booked when motivation is at its highest.

03

The idea

Una cosa bella.Something good.

Every day we read news we have no power over. Giving blood creates good news that depends only on you, and it reaches your phone like any other: your blood has arrived at the hospital. Not who: where. The campaign gives you that power, the app delivers the news, and the news invites you to make it happen again.

Social: Instagram and TikTok

Three short scripts. The first is the only moment in the campaign that plays on contrast: the good notification among the grey of everything else.

1 · The notification · 15 seconds

A young woman on the metro scrolls through notifications that take the shine off her day: a bill, a delayed train, a worrying headline. A different buzz: she stops, reads, smiles, and keeps it to herself.

«Oggi è successa una cosa bella. L'ho fatta succedere io.»“Something good happened today. I made it happen.”

2 · Good things today · 30 seconds

Using the small-joys list format already popular on social media, a creator lists the good things in their day: coffee made just right, the bus on time, the plant still alive. The last one is the AVIS notification.

«Questa l'ho fatta succedere io.»“I made this one happen.”

3 · Diary of a good thing · stories

A real AVIS donor tells the story of their week, one story a day. Monday: I donated, ten minutes. Wednesday: my blood is in the lab. Friday: it has arrived at the hospital. Last story: the date they can come back.

It ties in with AVIS's existing campaign «La gratuità ha il nostro volto» (“Giving for free has our face”): real donors stay at the centre.

06

The choices

Something good, not a duty

Blood campaigns often talk about need, emergencies and lives to save. This one talks about good news you make happen: no guilt, and the emotion belongs to the donor.

What comes after, not the first time

AVIS has already told the story of the first time and of young donors' faces. The open ground was what comes after: what happens to the blood, and what brings you back.

Member benefits, never prizes

Coupons and discounts for each donation work elsewhere, but in Italy donation is unpaid by law and AVIS stands by that. The app talks about rights, like paid leave, and about benefits for AVIS members.

Where, never who, never with what outcome

The notification only says what the data confirms: it has arrived at the hospital, it has gone to become a medicine. Never “you saved a life”, because we don't know that.

01

The context

Live music in small venues is a fragile economy: in about five years, 35% of Italian live music venues closed, Covid included (Rockit, 2024). Musicians and venues want the same thing, yet each sees the other as a risk. And the apps that already exist all promise the same thing: finding each other faster.

The musician

Wants a real audience, and to get paid.

Fears playing for free, or not getting paid at all.

Today sends messages that go unanswered and keeps hearing “how many people can you bring?”.

The venue

Wants a night that covers its costs.

Fears an empty room, a band that cancels or plays too loud.

Today gets dozens of identical pitches and takes on all the risk.

Things you hear in venues

“You get by, you don't make money.”

A venue owner, in l'Adige

“How many people are you bringing me?”

The first question, according to musicians on Rockit

“Make trouble and you won't play again.”

Musicians' silence, simulated interview

02

The insight

Musicians and venues want the same thing: a full room. But each hopes the other will be the one to fill it.

The conflict doesn't come from different priorities. It comes from the risk of filling the room, which nobody states and everyone tries to pass on to the other side. Money, expected audience and terms go unsaid, and every gig becomes a blind bet.

03

The idea

Everything said up front, risk split in half.

Pari doesn't set up a meeting between two enemies. It follows a gig from the agreement to settling up, with the same rules for both sides.

Before

The gig sheet

Fee, times, sound system, expected audience: it spells out who promotes what. Both sides confirm it.

During

Always an answer

Seven days to say yes, no or “let's change it”. Then the request expires: silence is no longer an answer.

After

Settled up,
for everyone

Musicians and venues confirm it went as agreed. Every gig that closes well builds trust.

E alla fine: siamo pari.And at the end: we're even.

04

The execution

The name

Pari

Alla pari:On equal terms: nobody is asking for a favour.

Siamo pari:We're even: the accounts are settled.

Pari come numero:Even, as in numbers: two sides, equal weight.

Only one rival made it to the final: Accordo, meaning both a chord and a deal. It lost because it's the name everyone would have thought of.

Positioning

For emerging musicians and live music venues in Rome, Pari is the app that puts in writing, before the gig, what usually goes unsaid. So the people who play and the people who host split the risk down the middle.

Three values

Clarity

Everything is said up front: money, times, audience. If it isn't written down, it doesn't count.

Reciprocity

The rules are the same for both sides: if you ask for an answer, you give one.

Respect

The stage and the bar are two trades. Neither is doing the other a favour.

Tone of voice

Pari talks like someone who has played in a band and worked behind the bar. It knows both sides, flatters no one, and calls money money. Every line has to work said out loud at the bar, at eleven at night.

The manifesto

Siamo pari.We're even.

Chi suona vuole una sala piena. Chi ha un locale vuole una sala piena. Eppure va sempre a finire allo stesso modo: uno chiede, l'altro concede.Musicians want a full room. Venues want a full room. And yet it always ends the same way: one side asks, the other grants.

Un messaggio che resta senza risposta. Un «vediamo come va». Un «portate gente». Un cachet che a fine serata diventa due birre e un panino.A message left unanswered. A “let's see how it goes”. A “bring people”. A fee that, by the end of the night, turns into two beers and a sandwich.

Non è cattiveria. È che certe cose non si dicono. Quanto si paga. Quanta gente si aspetta. Chi la porta. A che ora si stacca.It isn't malice. Some things just don't get said. How much it pays. How many people to expect. Who brings them. What time it ends.

Il palco ci mette la musica. Il bancone ci mette la sala. Il rischio si divide a metà. E a fine serata, comunque sia andata, ci si guarda e si dice: siamo pari.The stage brings the music. The choices

Pari, not Accordo

Accordo was the most obvious name, both a chord and a deal: which is exactly why everyone would have picked it. Pari says the same thing from the side of the relationship, and gives the brand its closing line.

Money named, always

Competing apps skirt around pay. Pari gives it a field of its own, because what goes unsaid is the problem. “Here, talking about money is normal” is worth more than any feature.

Two voices, no megaphone

The campaign doesn't talk down: it stages the dialogue between the stage and the bar. The brand simply closes it on equal terms.

Project 03 · Naming, concept and launch campaign

Niente da nascondere. Con Garbo.Nothing to hide. With Garbo (with grace).

The name and launch of Garbo, Smeg's first robot vacuum: an object designed to stay in sight, told in the brand's own voice.

Role

Strategy, naming, concept, copy

Brand

Smeg · first robot vacuum

Year

2026

Type

Personal project, not commissioned

The challenge

Smeg has never made a vacuum cleaner. The brief: launch its first robot in a market where everyone talks about power.

The idea in one line

Garbo, the robot that doesn't hide: a design object to leave out in the living room, like the FAB fridge in 1997.

What I did

Analysis of Smeg's voice on its website and social channels, insight, naming, concept and claim, manifesto, out of home and Instagram.

01

The context

The robot vacuum has made it into Italian homes. But it came in as a piece of tech: you choose it for suction power and battery life, then put it where it can't be seen.

€183M

value of the Italian market, up 14%

> 20%

of Italian households already own one

55.5%

Category communication talks about pascals, sensors and mapping. Some brands even publish guides on how to hide the robot and its dock. Open ground for a brand that has always sold shape and colour.

Sources: Italian trade press 2025–2026 (interviews with MOVA and Ecovacs, market data reported by Tom's Hardware and Tech Princess).

02

Smeg's voice

Before writing, I read the brand's website and social channels. I wanted a campaign consistent with how Smeg speaks today, not a new register.

It speaks like a design catalogue

Light, material, harmony. Finishes have a name and a description: Moonlight is a “neutral matt finish shaped by natural light”.

Short, crafted sentences

The “Name: claim” formula, the tricolon, mirrored sentences: «Design da vedere. Design da toccare.» (“Design to see. Design to touch.”)

The object before the function

The FAB was created to “leave behind the purpose of pure functionality”. Technology comes after aesthetics, never in the headlines.

Third person and “you”

In corporate copy the brand talks about itself in the third person; on social it addresses the reader, with questions and crisp answers: «Con Multiuse Grill SMEG, sì.» (“With the SMEG Multiuse Grill, yes.”)

Hence the rule for the robot: present to the eye, discreet in manner.

03

The insight

It's the only appliance we give a name to. And it's the only one we hide.

How we treat it

Like one of the family.

We call it by name, we watch it move out of curiosity, we clear its path under the sofa.

Georgia Tech: 21 out of 30 owners had given their robot a name.

How it's designed

Like a tool to put away.

A dark disc, made to disappear under furniture. A dock to tuck behind a door.

Some manufacturers publish guides on how to hide the robot and its dock.

The space for Smeg

Between how we live with it and how it's designed there's a gap: a robot to keep in sight.

Research data used as a behavioural signal, not as a statistic about the Italian market.

04

The idea

Niente da nascondere. Con Garbo.Nothing to hide. With Garbo (with grace).

A line that works twice: for the clean home, and for a robot that for the first time doesn't need to be hidden. The name completes it: con garbo means with grace and restraint. It's the campaign's only playful moment; everything else speaks in Smeg's registers.

The proof: Smeg has done it before

1997

The FAB fridge steps out of anonymity and becomes an icon.

2026

Isola: the kitchen that chooses to stand out instead of disappearing.

Today

Garbo, the brand's first robot: the third chapter of the same story.

05

The name

It's the appliance people give a name to. This time, the name comes from the people who make it.

Garbo

To do things con garbo: with grace, tact, restraint. It's “discreet in manner” in a single word. Two syllables, easy to say anywhere, and in Spanish it means elegance.

Product sign-off, like “Multiuse Grill SMEG”: Garbo SMEG.

Inside Smeg's naming system

Aesthetics

Italian words and places: Linea, Isola, Musa, Portofino.

Finishes

English names drawn from nature and light: Moonlight, Storm Blue, Emerald Green.

Products

Usually model codes. Only one became a name: the FAB. Garbo is a new kind of product, like the FAB was: Italian like the aesthetics, a character instead of a function.

Discarded

Andante

The robot's calm pace, in musical terms. Cultured, but long and already used by many brands.

A reference worth keeping

Garbo recalls Greta Garbo, the star who left Hollywood at her peak to live out of the spotlight. This Garbo does the opposite: it stays in sight.

The idea in one line

An editorial strategy in two stages: get the people who walk past it every evening to discover the Centrale, then give them a reason to come back.

What I did

From research to copy: data analysis, concept and hook. Planning an editorial strategy, three social series, a creator programme, a newsletter.

People who go in like the Centrale, and it's free for anyone who lives in Rome. The problem is something else: the people who hang out in the neighbourhood don't know it exists.

Sources: Roma Capitale statistical yearbooks, Culture chapter; Zètema customer satisfaction survey 2025 (120 interviews); Musei in Comune announcement on the new 2026 pricing.

02

The place

Ancient statues and industrial machines in the same room.

In 1997 the Capitoline Museums closed for renovation and moved hundreds of sculptures into Rome's first public power station, next to the diesel engines and boilers. It was meant to be a temporary exhibition. Since 2001 it has been a museum.

Elsewhere

Other power stations have become museums, from Tate Modern in London to the Power Station of Art in Shanghai. They house contemporary art or the history of energy.

Here

Around four hundred ancient sculptures, mosaics and portraits among turbines, engines and a fifteen-metre boiler. We couldn't find another like it.

The rule

The contrast between statues and machines is at the heart of most of the content. The slogan sparks the interest, the posts close the circuit.

03

The insight

People going out in Ostiense at night walk right past it. And they don't know what's behind that wall.

The neighbourhood

Full of under-35s.

Roma Tre university on the same street, the Gazometro, Teatro India, bars and street art. Ostiense is one of the youngest areas of the city.

The museum

Off their radar.

It isn't the price, the distance or the quality. It's that nobody has ever told them about it in their own voice.

And then

Romans who go there come with friends and by word of mouth. The path is already there.

04

The idea

Ma tu, la conosci la Centrale?So, do you know the Centrale?

A question between friends, not an institution's slogan. It opens the story and splits it into two stages: first we ask you, then you ask someone else.

For people who already know it. Every exhibition is a reason to come back, and to bring someone along.

Chosen

Ma tu, la conosci la Centrale?So, do you know the Centrale?

It's the question one person asks another. It has word of mouth built in.

Discarded

Ospiti da trent'anni.Guests for thirty years.

A nice historical fact, but “guests” made the statues feel lighter instead of giving them weight. It stays in the long-form piece.

Two stages. 77% are visiting for the first time, only 11% come back: first it needs to be discovered, then to give people a reason to return.

Contrast as a foundation, not an obligation. It's what makes the Centrale different, and it comes back in most of the content, while leaving room for exhibitions, events and the neighbourhood.

Three simple series. Easy to produce with a civic museum's resources.

A voice of its own. Today the Centrale speaks within a network of twenty museums: to be remembered, it needs a story of its own.

Personal project, not commissioned. No connection with the Centrale Montemartini, the Sovrintendenza Capitolina, Zètema or Roma Capitale. Data and historical facts from public sources. The audience is Roman, so the copy stays in Italian, with a translation.
